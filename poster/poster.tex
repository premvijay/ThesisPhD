    % Introduction
    \begin{block}{Dark Matter Haloes}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Over four-fifths of all matter in the standard paradigm of cosmology is dark, forming gravitationally collapsed structures called dark matter haloes that host the formation and evolution of galaxies.
        \item Understanding their nature and evolution is a multi-disciplinary task, from studying the particle nature of dark matter, the impact of baryonic astrophysical processes in and around galaxies, and the overall cosmology.
        \item Isolating the impact of galaxy formation and the associated astrophysical processes on the dark matter haloes is a pressing open problem.
    \end{itemize}
    \end{block}

    % Methods
    \begin{block}{Cosmological Simulations}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Cosmological simulations combine our knowledge of tiny fluctuations in the early Universe with late-time non-linear cosmological structures.
            \item The growth of structures driven by gravity is extensively studied in the absence of baryonic astrophysics through N-body simulations.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{block}
    \begin{block}{Galaxies in Cosmo Simulations}
        State-of-the-art simulations of galaxy formation such as EAGLE, IllustrisTNG, CAMELS employ subgrid prescriptions for various known astrophysical processes in hydrodynamical simulations of cosmological volumes.

    \begin{block}{Dark Matter Relaxation Response}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item As baryons within haloes condense and form galaxies, they can significantly change the phase-space distribution of the dark matter.
            \item In particular, the change in the halo-centric distances affects radial mass profiles of haloes that influence key observables such as the rotation curves and radial acceleration relations.
            \item This is usually modeled as adiabatic relaxation of dark matter in response to the net change in the gravitational potential due to galaxy formation.
            \item Consider a shell enclosing a \emph{dark matter} mass $M_i^d(r_i)$ at radius $r_i$ in the unrelaxed halo. After relaxation, the radius of the shell changes to $r_f$. By definition, the dark matter mass $M_f^d(r_f) = M_i^d(r_i)$.
            \item If angular momentum is conserved and the dark matter particle orbits stay circular, then,
            \begin{align*}
                r_i \,M_i(r_i) = r_f \,M_f(r_f) % 
                \implies 
                \frac{r_f}{r_i} = \frac{M_i(r_i)}{M_f(r_f)}\,. 
            \end{align*}
            Since this idealized model rarely predicts the response in simulations, quasi-adiabatic relaxation models have been proposed, considering the relaxation ratio $r_f/r_i$ as a function of the mass ratio $M_i/M_f$.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{block}

    \begin{block}{Relaxation in IllustrisTNG and EAGLE}
        We found that the relaxation relation (between $r_f/r_i$ and $M_i/M_f$) varies widely between haloes of different mass scales. However, a simple linear relation can accurately describe the relaxation, provided we assume an additional explicit dependence on the halo-centric distance.

    \end{block}

    \begin{block}{Relaxation dynamics}
        By tracing the evolutionary history of matched haloes, we could probe the dynamical evolution of the relaxation response of the dark matter halo to galaxy formation. 
        \begin{center}
            \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{plots/dynam_relxn/hal_relxn_offset_evolve.pdf}
        \end{center}
        The mean intercept parameter $q_0$ in the linear relaxation relation, describing the amount of relaxation $r_f/r_i-1 \equiv q_y$ for dark matter shells with no net change in the gravitational potential $M_i/M_f-1$, is shown in the left panel as a function of time (color indicates halo mass); also the model-independent quantity $q_y$ defined as the offset $r_f/r_i-1$ is shown in the right panel. Both $q_0$ and $q_y$ start at zero, become more negative initially, but then apparently revert slowly back to zero.
    \end{block}
    \begin{block}{Temporal connection with astrophysics}
        The relaxation offset was also found to strongly correlate with various halo and galaxy properties, in particular with quantities like star formation rate that is associated with supernovae and other stellar feedback output. 
        \begin{center}
            \includegraphics[width=.69\linewidth]{plots/dynam_relxn/Spea_correl_vs_shift_betw_q0-SFR_fullcorr.pdf}
        \end{center}
        Spearman-rank correlation shown above between $Q_0 \equiv -q_0$ and mean SFR across a variety of haloes over long periods of time at different final halo masses suggests that the offset is typically stronger following periods of high star formation activity. Additionally, we also found that the offset in the inner region is immediately affected by high SFR, while the outer regions are affected after around 2-3 Gyr. This likely explains the explicit dependence on the halo-centric distance.
    \end{block}
    \begin{block}{Role of Astrophysical feedback}
        Using the suite of CAMELS simulations with variation in feedback strengths, we found that the offset parameter $q_x$, which is inversely related to $q_0$, indeed strongly depends on the overall feedback flux from the galaxies. However, the parameters controlling the feedback burstiness and the wind speed have negligible impact on the relaxation offset as shown below.
